Service design in public mental health

The experience of low-income older adults in the Viva Vida program.

A qualitative study nested in a randomized controlled trial of Viva Vida, a WhatsApp-based psychosocial intervention for low-income older adults with depressive symptoms. I investigated the service components, the participant journey and the stakeholder map. The central finding changed what "the service" meant: users don't experience the six-week intervention that was designed, but a whole package that runs from recruitment to the last clinical assessment.

§ Research problem

What is it like to receive therapy by message when the phone belongs to your son?

Digital mental health tools are spreading, but they tend to be out of reach for low-income older adults because of cost, difficulty of use and lack of engagement. Viva Vida was running as a randomized controlled trial with more than a thousand participants recruited, with the clinical outcome measured by the PHQ-9 (Patient Health Questionnaire-9), a validated depression screening questionnaire. What didn't exist was qualitative evidence about the experience: which service components worked, where the journey broke down, and who, besides the user, kept access going.

§ My role

Lead qualitative researcher, inside the trial team

I designed the qualitative study, the interview guides and the three codebooks; conducted interviews alongside four other interviewers; and did all the coding, the journey synthesis and the stakeholder map. Running the trial and the intervention itself belonged to the hospital's Institute of Psychiatry research group.

§ Research design

Two sources, two moments, one codebook per question

A descriptive qualitative approach with a constructivist basis, reported according to COREQ (Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research), the international checklist for reporting qualitative research. Semi-structured phone interviews with a piloted guide, plus ongoing participant observation of the team that created and ran the intervention.

Approved by the hospital's ethics committee and registered in the Brazilian Clinical Trials Registry as part of the trial protocol. Tool-assisted transcription reviewed by transcribers; deductive thematic coding in Atlas.ti, following Bardin, using one codebook for each of the three research questions.

§ Sampling

Purposive, stratified on three axes

This wasn't a convenience sample: I built cells crossing sex, two age bands (60-69 and 70+) and improvement or no improvement on the PHQ-9 at the three-month assessment, with two clinical groups in parallel.

  • 48 interviews at the end of the program24 participants with subclinical symptoms and 24 with moderately severe symptoms. From 20 minutes to 3 hours long.
  • 7 interviews during the programParticipants recruited only for the qualitative analysis, interviewed four times over six weeks, until data saturation. Collection close to the experience, so the detail of what they had just heard wasn't lost.
  • 167 meetings as a participant observerApril 2021 to December 2022, focused on three axes: participants' difficulties, the creators' difficulties and the iterations made by the team.

§ Who this population is

Among program participants, 66.1% earned the minimum wage or less and 74.7% had only primary schooling. Most were aged 60-69 (84.2%); 69.5% reported hypertension and 41.3% diabetes. All were registered at a public primary care clinic. Recruiting here is not recruiting app users: part of my fieldwork was following 63 recruitment calls, of which 9 led to participation.

§ Finding 1 · Service components

The touchpoint hierarchy wasn't the one the design expected

The intervention was designed around the messages. For users, what mattered most was the channel and the human voice on the phone.

§ What worked
  • Phone callsThey became a source of support and a space to vent, including the calls that existed only to collect clinical outcomes.
  • Narrated audio2 to 5-minute stories recorded by professional actors created identification and engagement. Participants compared themselves to the character and reflected on what they enjoy doing.
  • WhatsApp as a channelAn entry point to digital inclusion. For some participants it was the only app they used, made possible by mobile plans that give unlimited WhatsApp.
  • The program's nameCame up in 35 interviews as a good fit for its purpose. The most frequent adjectives were nice, beautiful and interesting.
§ What failed
  • WhatsApp pollsThe sharpest pain point. They frightened participants with low digital literacy, who didn't know what would happen if they tapped an option.
  • Static imagesLow retention, especially among participants with more severe symptoms. Declining eyesight and text sizes below what is recommended for this age group.
  • The first messageArriving as a stranger on WhatsApp triggered scam alarms: participants blocked, ignored, went to check at their clinic or asked a son or daughter to look it up online.
  • Expecting a replyThe channel sets an expectation of conversation. Participants sent good-morning stickers to the program and waited for replies that never came.

§ Finding 2 · Journey

The service is not the six weeks of intervention.

For participants, the program starts with the recruitment call and ends with the last follow-up interview, months later. The trial's calls, which existed to measure outcomes, were experienced as part of the care. That repositions the object of design: you can't evaluate this service by looking only at what was designed as the intervention.

§ The five phases mapped
Phase 01

Before joining

A context of vulnerability: income, comorbidities, grief, isolation made worse by the pandemic. Participants explained their answers about symptoms through the social context they live in.

Phase 02

The invitation

A message from a stranger on WhatsApp, followed by a call hours later. A mix of distrust and curiosity; checking at the clinic or with relatives.

Phase 03

Recruitment interview

Many structured questions over the phone. The participant tries to chat between questions. The first moment they feel heard.

Phase 04

Receiving the intervention

8 to 11 messages a week for 6 weeks. A fork in the road: those who identify with the character do the activity and keep going; those who don't conclude the program isn't for them and stop listening.

Phase 05

Follow-up interviews

A month and a half and three and a half months later. Experienced as support and belonging, not as assessment.

§ Finding 3 · Stakeholders

Users and the team organized the ecosystem in incompatible ways

The intervention's creators thought in three blocks: research group, health system and participants. Users saw two: health system and support network, with no distinction between Viva Vida and the public health system. This mental model gap explains much of the frustrated expectations about continuity of care.

Children and relatives emerged as decisive actors: they checked whether the program was legitimate, taught how to open the audio and lent their own phones. Access to the intervention depended on people who weren't in the service design.

§ Access barriers

What prevented full use

Low digital literacy, technical and signal problems, hearing and visual accessibility, illiteracy and stigma around depression. On top of contextual barriers: the covid-19 pandemic, chronic illness and financial hardship.

§ Method decision

In-depth interviews, with surveys and focus groups ruled out.

Interviews were the only technique that could map the people around each older adult, build the journey in depth and surface points this population doesn't voice spontaneously in more rigid formats. The choice was expensive in analysis time: interviews of up to three hours, five interviewers to align and a pilot before starting.

§ What was left out

And why

  • SurveyLow literacy in the population studied. The answers wouldn't support the analysis.
  • Focus groupThe covid-19 health crisis, with participants in a high-risk group. Meeting in person was out of the question.
§ From finding to design decision

Four recommendations, each tied to a finding

§ Because access depended on others

Design for the support network, not just the user

Children and relatives were decisive for engagement and for building the digital skills that made use possible. Future designs should treat this network as an active part of the service, with its own materials and channel.

§ Because users and the team diverged

Distinguish the program from the health service

Users don't separate Viva Vida from the public health system. Without clear signals about what is the intervention and what is care, the program inherits expectations of continuity it can't meet.

§ Because the channel carries prior meaning

Explore WhatsApp's real limits

Users arrive with existing habits (a tool for conversation) and the platform has fixed features that limit clinical use. Calls, groups and other formats remain unexplored, despite the need for social connection that came up in the field.

§ Because the barriers were social, not just usability

Co-creation from the protocol, not just in evaluation

Involve patients, professionals and managers in designing similar interventions. The recommendation goes beyond the product: co-creative methods can shape the clinical protocol itself, from inclusion criteria to secondary outcomes.
